The opening line must prime the reader for drop-off, activation, bookings, signup completion, churn, paywall friction, retention, or conversion. It should feel like the sender actually checked the app or page.

Angle priority:
1. If there is a mobile app, app/onboarding evidence beats blog and generic website observations. Prefer Apple App Store evidence first when available, then Google Play, then screenshots, review complaints, onboarding permissions, signup requirement, paywall/subscription/access-code, then website/landing page.
2. Broken formatting on website/app, especially mobile formatting issues.
3. Conversion best-practice issues: hard-to-see CTA, unclear primary action, unnecessary clicks in onboarding, hidden signup/login options, checkout friction, or too many steps before value.
4. Weak testimonials or case studies without direct evidence, screenshots, quotes, or proof.
5. Broad positioning where the product is trying to serve too many audiences/use cases and a narrower niche may convert better.
6. Blog post angles are usually weak. Use a blog only if it directly exposes a positioning/onboarding/conversion issue and no stronger app/page surface exists.

UX validator rule:

Opening line rules:
- One sentence only.
- Ideally 22 to 38 words.
- Natural, sharp, and human.
- Open conversationally with a first-person surface check, for example "I was just checking out the [product] app...", "I opened the [product] app/listing...", "I was looking at the [product] App Store listing...", or "I was on the [product] website...".
- Say "the [product] app" or "the [product] website" instead of "[domain] website."
- Do not say "I downloaded the app", "I installed the app", or imply that the sender personally downloaded/installed it. Use "I opened", "I was checking out", or "I was looking at the App Store listing" unless a human reviewer supplied a manual app walkthrough note.
- Keep company or product names lowercase in the opening line when the supplied name is lowercase. Do not title-case a company name just to make it look cleaner.
- Lead with a specific bug, friction point, unclear step, dead button, onboarding issue, booking issue, paywall issue, weak proof point, or unclear first action when evidence supports it.
- Tie the observation to a concrete outcome such as user drop-off, activation, conversion, retention, bookings, signup completion, first-session churn, or paywall conversion.
- Make the line flow naturally into the required_next_sentence supplied in the payload.
- Prefer a mild hypothesis when evidence supports it: "I'd guess...", "I bet...", "that probably...", "that could be costing...", "that feels like a place where..."
- No generic praise.
- No filler words such as powerful, impressive, interesting, or innovative.
- Do not use "I love", "I was impressed by", "Hope you're well", "smooth experience", or "make the experience smoother".
- No em dashes.
- No robotic phrases.
- No unsupported claims.
- Must feel written for this specific company or person.
- If recipient role is relevant, lightly tailor the line to that role without forcing it.
- Prefer the strongest evidence in this order: actual app/onboarding observation, concrete bug/friction point, Apple App Store listing/review detail, Google Play listing detail, website flow detail, person-level LinkedIn observation supplied by reviewer, recent product/news note, competitor/context note.
- For app-first products, prefer app/onboarding and app-store/review/screenshot evidence over blog/site observations unless the site has a clearer current conversion bug.
- Override that order when the evidence contains one of the client's priority issues: broken formatting, CTA visibility, unnecessary clicks, weak proof, or broad positioning.
- Suitable as the first line of a cold email.
- Do not stack multiple observations. One surface, one friction point, one outcome hypothesis.
